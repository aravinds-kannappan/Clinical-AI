\section{Introduction}
Clinical monitoring systems do more than assign a risk score. They determine which observations are processed, when an alert appears, who receives it and whether someone acts before an opportunity to intervene has passed. Each decision changes the practical meaning of model performance. A score can rank patients well while producing an unmanageable stream of reviews. An alert can receive a favorable benchmark reward yet arrive outside the time window used to define a successful human assessment. A consent restriction can remove an input without removing information already cached in a feature summary or recurrent state.

These distinctions motivate our research question: can a healthcare AI monitoring pipeline remain useful while sensitive data flows, model errors and consequential decisions are governed by explicit consent and effective human oversight? We treat this as a conjunction of requirements. Predictive discrimination, timely event detection, review workload, permitted information flow and correct human action are separate quantities. Evidence for one cannot silently stand in for another.

Sepsis monitoring provides a useful test case because repeated clinical observations, rare events and time-sensitive alerts make these requirements interact. The PhysioNet/CinC 2019 challenge supplied a common benchmark and an explicitly time-dependent utility function \citep{reyna2020}. External validation and implementation studies show why evaluation must extend beyond a development-set discrimination score: model performance can deteriorate in another setting, and clinicians' engagement with alerts is itself an empirical question \citep{wong2021,trews}.

We investigate a simple operational proposal: reduce repeated alerts by requesting at most one review per patient. The proposal appears attractive because threshold crossings remain nested as the threshold decreases. The relevant clinical loss, however, need not be nested. If the first alert consumes the only review before the valid warning window, lowering the threshold can convert a timely review into a missed incident. This distinction invalidates an unqualified application of monotone-loss conformal risk control to timely first-alert loss.

Our methodological contribution is a precise treatment of that policy mismatch. We characterize population feasibility through pre-window and in-window score maxima, then apply simultaneous binomial confidence bounds to a validation-frozen policy grid. The output has three meanings: a policy is certified to meet both targets, every policy on the grid is excluded, or the sample is insufficient to decide. The confidence machinery follows existing risk-control work; the contribution lies in matching it to the incident-review endpoint and making the operational assumptions explicit.

We evaluate the construction on all 40,336 public PhysioNet patients using tuned logistic regression, histogram gradient boosting and a unidirectional GRU. Patient-level splits separate training, validation, calibration and testing. Additional analyses examine simulated consent changes, hospital-specific recalibration, overlapping demographic groups, noisy review queues and structural review-path checks. The strongest models improve discrimination, but all locally calibrated first-alert grids fail the declared 5\% conditional missed-event target. The resulting negative answer is specific: this model and policy family does not demonstrate the conjunction in our research question. It does not establish that a different monitoring or continuing-review policy cannot do so.

\section{Related work}
\subsection{Clinical prediction, alert utility and deployment}
The PhysioNet challenge was designed to make early sepsis prediction comparable across cohorts, clinical variables and evaluation procedures \citep{reyna2020}. Its utility rewards prediction at different times relative to onset and penalizes false alarms. That makes it more informative than a single threshold-free ranking metric, but it still evaluates a prescribed prediction representation. Our once-per-patient review count and strict first-review window are additional estimands, not replacements for the challenge's original definition.

Clinical time-series benchmarks have established the value of comparing linear and recurrent models within a common preprocessing and evaluation framework \citep{harutyunyan2019}. Missingness is also informative: the fact that a measurement was ordered can predict outcomes, independently of the recorded value. GRU-D explicitly represents missingness and time intervals \citep{che2018}. Our GRU is a simpler baseline using causal summaries and observation counts; it does not implement learned decay or reproduce GRU-D's architecture. This distinction matters when interpreting the modest model search as evidence about a policy family rather than a comprehensive search for the best sepsis predictor.

Discrimination, probability quality and clinical usefulness also differ. Precision--recall analysis is particularly informative when positives are rare \citep{saito2015}; calibration studies emphasize that apparently strong ranking does not ensure reliable risk probabilities \citep{vancalster2019}. We therefore tune using validation average precision and report AUROC, average precision, Brier score and challenge utility. Class-weighted training can improve the ordering of cases while changing the scale of the predicted probabilities, so we do not interpret the raw weighted scores as calibrated clinical risks.

The external validation of the Epic Sepsis Model examined both predictive performance and the burden of alternative alerting strategies \citep{wong2021}. The TREWS adoption study examined whether clinicians evaluated alerts and how their interactions related to treatment timing \citep{trews}. These studies motivate our separation between an alert being emitted and a correct action being completed. They do not supply the active service-time distribution for our simulated reviewers; response delay includes workflow and availability as well as time spent on the assessment.

\subsection{Risk control and constrained policy selection}
Conformal risk control bounds an expected loss under exchangeability and the required regularity of a nested loss family \citep{crc}. Risk-controlling prediction sets and Learn then Test instead provide high-probability statements about candidate procedures, with selection justified by valid tests or simultaneous confidence statements \citep{rcps,ltt}. Pareto Testing addresses the efficient selection of configurations subject to multiple risk constraints \citep{pareto}. Our finite-grid construction is deliberately elementary: exact binomial intervals and a union bound provide a transparent reference method for a non-monotone first-alert loss.

The distinction between the guarantees is consequential. An expectation over calibration and a future patient is not the same object as a high-probability upper bound on the population risk of a fitted rule. Likewise, failing to pass a test is not evidence that every candidate is infeasible. We use lower bounds to support the latter statement and retain an undetermined outcome when neither direction is established. We make no claim that the underlying binomial, union-bound or importance-weight arguments are new.

Recent clinical selective-prediction work combines conformal prediction with cost-aware deferral and evaluates group-conditional and shifted settings \citep{kwon2026}. Such work is closely related in motivation. Our loss is a missed incident among event-positive patients, and our capacity variable is the number of patient reviews. Prediction-set coverage, error on retained cases and timely first review are different random variables, so a guarantee for one does not transfer automatically to the others. Under hospital shift, weighted conformal methods further require a relationship between source and target distributions \citep{weighted}; an estimated domain-classifier ratio alone does not verify that relationship.

\subsection{Consent and meaningful oversight}
Dynamic consent treats permission as an ongoing interaction in which participants can manage preferences as data use evolves \citep{kaye2014}. We model one technical consequence of changing permissions: which inputs and derived state are available to a predictor. This supports controlled sensitivity experiments but omits comprehension, communication, preference formation and the institutional process through which consent is obtained.

Meaningful human control has also been formulated in terms of systems responding to relevant human reasons and outcomes remaining attributable to responsible people \citep{santoni2018}. A review node in an information graph captures only a routing requirement. It says neither that a reviewer has enough information nor that the reviewer has time, competence or authority to act. Our graph checks and stochastic reviewer model address distinct parts of that larger process, while prospective human evaluation remains outside the present study.
